
 **What is a White Dwarf?**
- **Definition:** The remaining core of a dead low- to intermediate-mass star (< 8 M☉).  
- **Support Mechanism:** **Electron degeneracy pressure** – quantum mechanical effect from the Pauli exclusion principle.
- **No Fusion:** Cooling occurs over time; they emit residual heat but have no significant fusion source.

---
**Properties**
- **Mass:** Up to ~1.4 M☉ (Chandrasekhar limit)  
- **Size:** Comparable to Earth (~10,000 km radius)  
- **Density:** Extremely high; a solar-mass white dwarf is ~1 million times denser than Earth.
- **Mass–Radius Relationship:**  
  - More massive → smaller radius  
  - Reason: Stronger gravity compresses the star; degeneracy pressure supports it rather than thermal pressure.
- **Quantum Mechanics:**  
  - Electrons move faster as the star is squeezed (uncertainty principle)  
  - Maximum mass occurs when electrons approach the speed of light (relativistic limit).
---
 **Famous Example**
- **Sirius B:** Companion to Sirius A (brightest star in the sky)  
  - A classic white dwarf observable with powerful telescopes.
---
**Cooling and Evolution**
- Starts **very hot**, but no fusion sustains temperature
- Cools gradually over billions of years
- Ends as a faint, cold, dense object
---
 **The Chandrasekhar Limit**
- **Maximum stable mass:** 1.4 M☉  
- **Beyond this limit:**  
  - Electron degeneracy cannot support the core  
  - Collapse occurs → forms a **neutron star** or triggers a **Type Ia supernova** if in a binary system  
- Explains why **high-mass stars** cannot leave behind white dwarfs
---
**Key Takeaways:**
1. White dwarfs are quantum-supported stellar remnants.  
2. More massive white dwarfs are smaller due to gravity compressing the degenerate electrons.  
3. 1.4 M☉ is the ultimate limit; beyond that, the white dwarf is unstable.
---
**Close Binary Systems**
- Many stars exist in **binary systems**, and some are **close binaries**.  
- **White dwarfs** in close binaries can **accrete mass** from a companion star.  
- Companion may be a massive star or a low-mass star that has become a **giant**.
---
**Mass Transfer and Accretion Disk Formation**
- **Mechanism:**  
  - Companion expands → white dwarf’s gravity captures material  
  - Material falls towards the white dwarf  
- **Angular Momentum:**  
  - Infalling gas cannot fall straight in due to conservation of angular momentum  
  - Forms an **accretion disk** around the white dwarf  
- **Friction/Viscosity:**  
  - Transfers angular momentum outward  
  - Inner material spirals inward, heats up, emits light  
- **Key Concept:**  
  - Without friction, gas would **orbit indefinitely** (no inward motion)
---
**Nova Explosions**
- **Accreted Material:** Hydrogen from companion star accumulates on the white dwarf’s surface  
- **Trigger:** High pressure and temperature at the white dwarf surface ignite **hydrogen fusion**  
- **Outcome:**  
  - **Nova:** Sudden, explosive brightening of the star  
  - Ejects material into space, visible as gas blobs or shells  
  - Recurrent events possible as long as the white dwarf continues to accrete fresh material  
**Example:** Hubble image of T Pyxidis nova ejecta.
---
**Key Points**
1. Accretion disks form because of angular momentum conservation and cooling.  
2. Friction in the disk is essential for inward flow and heating.  
3. Fresh hydrogen accreting onto a white dwarf can reignite fusion, causing **novae**, not supernovae.  
4. Novae are much less energetic than supernovae; the white dwarf remains intact.
