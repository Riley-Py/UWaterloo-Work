
## 1. What is a White Dwarf?
- **Definition:** The remaining core of a dead low- to intermediate-mass star (< 8 M☉).  
- **Support Mechanism:** **Electron degeneracy pressure** – quantum mechanical effect from the Pauli exclusion principle.
- **No Fusion:** Cooling occurs over time; they emit residual heat but have no significant fusion source.

---
**Properties**
- **Mass:** Up to ~1.4 M☉ (Chandrasekhar limit)  
- **Size:** Comparable to Earth (~10,000 km radius)  
- **Density:** Extremely high; a solar-mass white dwarf is ~1 million times denser than Earth.
- **Mass–Radius Relationship:**  
  - More massive → smaller radius  
  - Reason: Stronger gravity compresses the star; degeneracy pressure supports it rather than thermal pressure.
- **Quantum Mechanics:**  
  - Electrons move faster as the star is squeezed (uncertainty principle)  
  - Maximum mass occurs when electrons approach the speed of light (relativistic limit).

---
 **Famous Example**
- **Sirius B:** Companion to Sirius A (brightest star in the sky)  
  - A classic white dwarf observable with powerful telescopes.

---

## 4. Cooling and Evolution
- Starts **very hot**, but no fusion sustains temperature
- Cools gradually over billions of years
- Ends as a faint, cold, dense object

---

## 5. The Chandrasekhar Limit
- **Maximum stable mass:** 1.4 M☉  
- **Beyond this limit:**  
  - Electron degeneracy cannot support the core  
  - Collapse occurs → forms a **neutron star** or triggers a **Type Ia supernova** if in a binary system  
- Explains why **high-mass stars** cannot leave behind white dwarfs

---

**Key Takeaways:**
1. White dwarfs are quantum-supported stellar remnants.  
2. More massive white dwarfs are smaller due to gravity compressing the degenerate electrons.  
3. 1.4 M☉ is the ultimate limit; beyond that, the white dwarf is unstable.
