
**Light carries energy**  
- Sunlight **warms** your skin → light must carry **energy**.  
- Thermal energy increases when light is absorbed.  
- Energy flow from light is measured in **watts** (1 W = 1 J/s).
**Light has color**  
- White light is a mix of many colors (the **visible spectrum**).  
- Rainbow colors: **violet → blue → green → yellow → red**.  
- Beyond visible light are **invisible** regions (infrared, UV, etc.).  
- As we’ll learn later, **color corresponds to frequency** of light.

---
 **How Light Interacts with Matter**
**Emission**  
- Matter can produce light.  
- Example: a light bulb filament or a hot piece of metal glowing red.
 **Absorption**  
- Matter takes in light’s energy.  
- Sunlight melting snow is absorption → energy goes into warming the snow.
 **Transmission**  
- Light passes through material.  
- Transparent materials (like glass) transmit most wavelengths → they appear clear.
 **Reflection & Scattering**  
- Light can bounce off or be redirected.
---
**Reflection vs. Scattering**
 **Reflection**
- Light bounces off a surface at the **same angle** it came in.  
- Mirror example:  
  - Incoming at 50° → reflected at 50°.  
  - Angle is predictable and stays in one direction.
**Scattering**
- Light is redirected into **many different directions**.  
- A movie screen scatters light so that viewers anywhere can see the image.  
- Everyday scattering explains why most objects do not look like mirrors.
**Key difference:**  
- *Reflection preserves direction; scattering redistributes direction.*

---
 **Light as Wave & Particle**
- Light shows **wave–particle duality**.
- Particles of light are **photons** (massless).
- All matter can act as waves or particles (quantum mechanics).
 **What Is a Wave?**
- Waves carry **energy**, not matter.
- Examples: water waves, sound waves, light waves.
 **Key Properties**
- **Wavelength (λ):** distance between crests.
- **Frequency (f):** vibrations per second (Hz).
- **Wave Speed:**
	$v = \lambda f$
---
 **Electromagnetic Waves**
- Light = oscillating **electric & magnetic fields**.
- Interacts with charged particles (especially electrons).
- All EM waves satisfy:
	  $c = \lambda f$
  where $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$

 Note: The speed of light is *defined*, not measured.

---
 **Wavelength–Frequency Examples**
Using $c = \lambda f$:
- **λ = 1 cm → f = 30 GHz**
- **λ = 0.5 cm → f = 60 GHz**
- **λ = 0.25 cm → f = 120 GHz**
---
**Photon Energy (Quantization)**
- Waves have discrete energy packets (photons).
  $E = h f$
- $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$
- Higher **frequency** → higher **photon energy**.
---
 **Polarization**
- Polarization = direction of light’s **electric field** oscillation.
- Reflection & scattering can polarize light.
- Polarized sunglasses block certain orientations of light.
- Sky light is polarized due to scattering.
---
**EM Spectrum**
- The electromagnetic (EM) spectrum spans a huge range of **wavelengths** and **frequencies**.
- Humans see only a tiny portion: the **visible spectrum** (~400–700 nm).
- Outside the visible range lie many types of EM waves with different properties and uses.

---
**Order of the Electromagnetic Spectrum**
From **longest wavelength** / **lowest energy** → **shortest wavelength** / **highest energy**:
**Radio Waves**
   - Wavelength: meters to centimeters
   - Notes: Used for radio, WiFi, cell phones; many wavelengths blocked by Earth’s ionosphere.
**Microwaves**
   - Wavelength: cm to mm
 **Infrared (IR)**
   - “Beyond red”
   - Emitted by warm objects (including people)
**Visible Light**
   - Wavelength: ~400–700 nm
   - ~1 micron scale ≈ $10^{-6}\ \text{m}$
 **Ultraviolet (UV)**
   - “Beyond violet”
   - Higher energy, shorter wavelengths
**X-Rays**
**Gamma Rays**
   - Shortest wavelengths: $10^{-10} {to} 10^{-12} \text{m}$
   - Highest energies

---
**Wavelength, Frequency, and Energy**
 **Speed of light relationship:**
   $c = \lambda f$
**Photon energy:**
$E = h f$ or $E = h * {c\over \lambda}$ 
Where:  
- $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$  
- $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$
 **Electron Volts (eV)**
- Energy can be expressed in **electron volts**:
  
$1\ \text{eV} = \frac{E_{\text{photon}}}{e}$
  
  where \(e\) is the electron charge $e=1.602×10^{−19}$ .

---
**What Wavelengths Can Probe**
- **Shorter wavelengths** can probe **smaller objects**.  
  - Example: electron microscopes → probe molecules (visible wavelengths too large).
- **Longer wavelengths** cannot resolve small-scale structures.

---
 **Humans as EM Emitters**
- People absorb visible light from the Sun.
- Biological processes warm the body → we emit **infrared radiation**.
- Scattered visible light comes from skin/clothing, but thermal emission is IR.
---
 **Matter**
- Matter is made of **atoms** → **molecules** → **matter**.
- Typical scale:
  - Atom: ~$10^{-10}$ m (hydrogen atom example)
  - Nucleus: ~$10^{-15}$ m (100,000× smaller than atom)
- Most of the atom’s volume is **electron cloud** (quantum-mechanical, “smeared out” electrons).

---
 **Atomic Structure**
- **Nucleus:** contains **protons** (+ charge) and **neutrons** (neutral)
  - Held together by **nuclear forces**
- **Electrons:** orbit around the nucleus, mostly define the atom’s size
- **Atomic Number (Z):** number of protons
  - Determines element identity
  - For neutral atoms, number of electrons = Z
- **Atomic Mass (A):** number of protons + neutrons
  - Hydrogen: Z = 1, A = 1  
  - Helium: Z = 2, A = 4  
  - Carbon-12: Z = 6, A = 12

---
**Molecules**
- Formed by **binding multiple atoms**.
- Examples: H₂O, CO₂, CH₄

---
 **Isotopes**
- Atoms of the **same element** (same Z) but **different number of neutrons**.
- Examples:
  - Helium-3: 2 protons + 1 neutron
  - Helium-4: 2 protons + 2 neutrons
  - Carbon-12: 6 protons + 6 neutrons
  - Carbon-13: 6 protons + 7 neutrons
  - Carbon-14: 6 protons + 8 neutrons (radioactive → used in **carbon dating**)
- Isotopes are important in **dating materials** in astrophysics, geophysics, and archaeology.

---
**Common Phases**
**Solid**
   - Particles tightly packed, fixed positions
   - Can be **crystalline** (ordered) or **amorphous** (disordered)
   - Examples: ice, iron, glass
**Liquid**
   - Particles touch but **move freely**
   - Shape adapts to container
   - Viscosity depends on chemical bonds
   - Examples: water, honey
 **Gas**
   - Particles far apart, mostly non-interacting
   - Fills the container
   - Examples: water vapor, air
**Plasma**
   - Ionized gas: electrons separated from nuclei
   - Found in stars, fluorescent lights
   - At extremely high temperatures → fully ionized nuclei
**Note:** On Earth, we mostly see solids, liquids, and gases; plasma is rare naturally.
---
 **Phase Transitions**
- Changes between phases depend on **temperature** and **pressure**:
  - **Low T → solid**  
  - **Moderate T → liquid**  
  - **High T → gas**  
  - **Very high T → plasma**
- **Phase coexistence:** Some phases can exist together at specific T & P (e.g., ice + water).
---
**Discrete Energy Levels in Atoms**
- Atoms (and molecules) have **discrete energy levels** due to quantum mechanics.
- Example: **Hydrogen atom**
  - Electron bound to proton
  - **Ground state:** minimum energy (0 eV reference)
  - Excited states: specific energies (e.g., 10.2 eV, 12.1 eV, 12.8 eV)
- **Unit:** 1 eV = \(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}\)  
  - Energy gained by an electron moving through 1 volt

---
**Photon Absorption**
- A photon can only be absorbed if its energy **matches the energy difference between levels**:
$E_{\text{photon}} = E_{\text{higher}} - E_{\text{lower}}$
- Photon energies **between levels cannot be absorbed**.
- Example:
  - Hydrogen cannot absorb a photon of 5 eV → no matching level
  - Photon of 10.2 eV → absorbed → electron moves to next level

---
 **Ionization**
- If photon energy ≥ **ionization energy**, atom loses an electron → **plasma**
  - Hydrogen ionization energy: 13.6 eV (UV photon)
- Visible light (~1–3 eV) usually cannot ionize hydrogen
---
 **Excited Atoms**
- Electron in an excited state can:
  1. Absorb another photon → move to higher level  
  2. Emit photon → drop to a lower level
- Photon emission and absorption are **quantized**, discrete events
---
**What Is a Spectrum?**
- **Spectrum:** Intensity of light as a function of wavelength (or frequency)
- Measured by:
  - Prism
  - Diffraction grating
  - Spectrometer
- Reveals properties of the light source and intervening matter
---
**Three Basic Types of Spectra**
**Continuous Spectrum**
- **Source:** Hot, dense object (e.g., incandescent light bulb, stars)
- **Properties:**
  - Light emitted at **all wavelengths**
  - Smooth distribution, no discrete features
  - Peak wavelength depends on **temperature** (Wien’s Law)
- Example: Sun’s photosphere (dense, hot plasma)
 **Emission Line Spectrum**
- **Source:** Hot, **low-density** gas cloud
- **Properties:**
  - Light emitted at **discrete wavelengths** corresponding to **atomic/molecular transitions**
  - Lines correspond to **photon energies**: $E = h f$
  - Hotth does not always mean high temperature — excitation can be electrical or other mechanisms
- Example: Hydrogen gas lamp, diffuse nebulae
**Absorption Line Spectrum**
- **Source:** Continuous background light passes through **cold, dilute gas**
- **Properties:**
  - Light is **absorbed at discrete wavelengths** matching atomic/molecular transitions
  - Appears as **dark lines** on top of a continuous spectrum
  - Reveals **composition of the intervening gas**
- Example: Stellar spectra showing hydrogen, sodium, calcium absorption lines
---
**Chemical Fingerprints**
- Each **atom/molecule** has a **unique set of energy levels**
- Each **transition** corresponds to a unique:
  - Photon energy $E = h f$
  - Frequency $f$
  - Wavelength $lambda$
- Matching **multiple spectral lines** gives confidence in identification
  - Like matching multiple features in a fingerprint
- Works for atoms or molecules as long as some electrons are bound
  - **Completely ionized gas** → no spectral lines → cannot identify composition

---

**Balmer Lines (Hydrogen Example)**
- Transitions from higher energy levels to **n=2** produce **Balmer series**
- Emission lines: hot hydrogen gas
- Absorption lines: cold hydrogen gas in front of background light
- Wavelengths depend on **initial energy level**:
  - Higher starting level → shorter wavelength (higher energy photon)

---

**Emission vs Absorption**

| Spectrum Type | Source | Appearance | Example |
|---------------|--------|-----------|---------|
| Emission      | Hot, excited, low-density gas | Bright lines over dark background | Hydrogen lamp, nebulae |
| Absorption    | Cold, dilute gas in front of continuous source | Dark lines on bright background | Solar photosphere, planetary atmospheres |

- **Continuum spectrum** often acts as backlight
- Absorption lines give **composition of intervening gas**

---
 **Molecular Spectra**
- Molecules have **rotational & vibrational energy levels**
- Transitions usually in **infrared & microwave**
- Examples:
  - Microwave ovens excite water molecules → heat food
  - Infrared absorption/emission reveals molecular composition in atmospheres
---
**Observational Applications**
- **Solar spectrum:** continuum from dense sun + absorption lines from cooler gas
- **Planetary spectra:**
  - Reflected sunlight → peaks near visible yellow
  - Thermal emission → infrared peak
  - Ultraviolet → ionosphere processes (e.g., ozone)
  - Absorption lines reveal molecules in atmosphere (e.g., water vapor)
- **Element identification:** sodium, hydrogen, iron, neon, etc.
- **Molecules identification:** H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, etc.
---
**Thermal Radiation**
- **All large, dense objects emit thermal radiation**
- **Spectrum depends only on temperature** (photons randomize past history)
- **Hotter objects:**
  - Emit more light per unit area at all wavelengths
  - Photons have **higher average energy**
  - Peak wavelength shifts to **shorter wavelengths**

---

**Examples of Peak Wavelengths**

| Object            | Temperature (K) | Peak Wavelength | Color / Band       |
|------------------|----------------|----------------|------------------|
| Human body       | 300            | ~10 µm         | Infrared         |
| Red star         | 3000           | Infrared       | Red              |
| Sun              | 5800           | ~0.5 µm       | Yellow/Visible  |
| Hot blue star    | >10,000        | UV             | Blue/Violet      |

---
**Stefan-Boltzmann Law**
- **Total energy emitted per unit area**:
$F = \sigma T^4$
- Where:
  - $F$ = energy flux (W/m²)
  - $T$ = temperature in Kelvin
  - $\sigma= 5.7 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W/m²·K⁴}$ (Stefan-Boltzmann constant)
- **Total power emitted**: multiply by surface area \(A\)
---
**Wien’s Law**
- **Wavelength of maximum emission**:
$\lambda_{\text{max}} = \frac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}}{T}$
- Where \(T\) is in Kelvin
- Determines **color/peak wavelength** of thermal radiation

---
**Applications**
- Measure **surface temperature** of stars or planets from peak wavelength
- Determine **total energy output** using Stefan-Boltzmann law
- Compare stars/planets: hotter objects → more energetic, bluer light; cooler objects → redder or infrared
---
**Reflected Sunlight**
- Continuous spectrum similar to the Sun
- Some wavelengths absorbed → alters color (e.g., blue light absorption → redder appearance)
- **Blackbody/thermal radiation** shape: smooth peak at wavelength corresponding to temperature

---
**Thermal Radiation in Infrared**
- Planet emits its own thermal radiation based on temperature
- **Example:** Infrared peak corresponds to ~225 K for Mars
- Can be affected by **reflectivity** and surface properties
---
**Atmospheric Absorption**
- Certain wavelengths absorbed by atmospheric molecules
- Example: **CO₂ absorption** in infrared for Mars
- Each molecule has **unique spectral fingerprints** in IR and microwave

---
**Ultraviolet Emission**
- Originates from **upper atmosphere/ionosphere**
- Requires **energetic processes**, e.g., solar wind or charged particle interactions
- Excites atoms/molecules to high-energy states → UV emission lines

---
 **Example: Mars Spectrum**
- Red appearance due to reflected sunlight + absorption of blue light
- Infrared peak → thermal emission of surface (~225 K)
- CO₂ absorption lines → atmosphere composition
- UV emission → upper atmosphere excited by solar wind
---
**Basics**
- Motion of an object causes **shift in spectral lines** (absorption or emission lines)
- **Wavelength change** tells us motion along the line of sight
- **Redshift**: Wavelengths **increase** → object moving **away**
- **Blueshift**: Wavelengths **decrease** → object moving **toward**

---
**How it Works**
- For an emission spectrum:
  - Original lab wavelength: λ₀
  - Observed wavelength: λ
  - **Shift**: Δλ = λ − λ₀
  - Redshift: Δλ > 0, Blueshift: Δλ < 0
- Relative shift proportional to velocity along line of sight (v):
  - Approximation for non-relativistic speeds:  
    $\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda_0} \approx \frac{v}{c}$
  - c = speed of light

---
**Line Ratios**
- Ratios of spectral lines **do not change** under Doppler shift
- Identifies the **element/molecule**
- Amount of shift → gives **speed along line of sight**
---
 **Vector Considerations**
- Doppler shift depends only on **line-of-sight velocity component**
- Perpendicular motion has negligible effect
- Example:
  - Star moving **away** → redshift
  - Star moving **toward** → blueshift
  - Star moving **perpendicular** → almost no shift
 **Rotation and Spectral Line Broadening**
- **Rotation of a star or object** causes different parts of the surface to move at different velocities relative to us.
  - Part **moving toward observer** → blueshifted
  - Part **moving away from observer** → redshifted
  - Part **perpendicular to line of sight** → no shift
- **Effect on spectral lines**:
  - The superposition of all these shifts **broadens the spectral line**
  - Faster rotation → **broader lines**
  - Slower rotation → **narrower lines**
- **Usage**:
  - Measure the **width of spectral lines** to determine **rotation speed**
  - Can reveal **kinematic structure** of stars or other astrophysical objects
