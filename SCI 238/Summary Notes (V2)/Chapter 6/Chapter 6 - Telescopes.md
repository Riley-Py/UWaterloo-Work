**Light and Wave Propagation**
- Light is a wave; **wavefronts** are surfaces of constant phase.
- **Direction of propagation** = perpendicular to wavefront.
- Point source → spherical wavefronts.
- Far away → wavefronts appear approximately **planar**, rays nearly parallel.
**Human Eye**
- **Main parts**: lens, pupil, retina, optic nerves.
- Light passes through **lens**, focused onto **retina**.
- Lens bends light via **refraction** (speed change in medium → direction change).
- Focus depends on **distance** of object:
  - Lens changes shape (accommodation) for near/far objects.
  - Light from a single point forms a **point on the retina**.
- Image on retina is **inverted**, brain corrects it.
**Refraction Examples**
- Stick in water appears bent.
- Sun looks distorted near horizon due to atmospheric refraction.
- Convex lens focuses parallel light rays to a point (focal point).
**Cameras**
- Operate on same principle as eyes:
  - **Lens** focuses light.
  - **Shutter** controls exposure time (eye: eyelid; camera: mechanical/electronic).
  - **CCD** (Charge-Coupled Device) replaces retina: absorbs photons, converts to electrical signals.
- Older cameras used **film** (chemical reactions), modern digital cameras use CCDs.
- Image formation:
  - Objects off principal axis form images on the **focal plane**.
  - Focal plane = where light from distant object is focused.
  - Convex lenses create inverted images; concave lenses cannot form real images.
**Telescopes**
- **Optical telescopes** use refraction (lenses) or reflection (mirrors) to focus light.
- **CCD detectors** capture images electronically.
- Professional telescopes combine optics and software:
  - Optical system forms primary image.
  - Software refines, enhances, or combines multiple images.
- **Radio telescopes**:
  - Longer wavelength → photons easier to measure electronically.
  - Can do much of imaging with software without physical lens.
---
 **Professional Telescopes: The “Giant Eyes” of Astronomy**
Professional telescopes are essentially **huge, highly engineered versions** of the same optical principles used in eyes and cameras. Because of their enormous size, additional technologies are needed to operate them effectively.

---
**Two Most Important Properties of Telescopes**
 **Light-Collecting Area**
- Determines **how much light** the telescope gathers.
- Larger collecting area → **better ability to detect faint objects** (e.g., distant galaxies, dim stars).
- Your eye’s light-collecting area is about a **square millimeter**.
- Large professional telescopes have mirrors **meters across**, giving **millions** of times more collecting area.
- For a circular telescope mirror:
  $A = \pi \left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2$ 
**Why this matters:**
- Fainter objects emit fewer photons.
- A bigger telescope gathers more photons per second → clearer detections and lower noise.

---
 **Angular Resolution**
- Tells you **how much detail** the telescope can resolve.
- Larger telescopes can distinguish **smaller angular separations** on the sky.
- Important for studying:
  - Star surfaces  
  - Shapes of galaxies  
  - Close binary stars  
  - Fine structure in nebulae  
**Key idea:** Bright but tiny features need good resolution, not necessarily huge brightness.
---
**What Telescopes *Do Not* Do**
- People often ask: “How far can a telescope see?”
  - This is *not* a fixed property. It depends on the **brightness of the object**.
  - A telescope can see:
    - a bird at 1 km  
    - a galaxy at 10 billion light-years  
  - What matters are the **two properties above**, not distance.
---
**Diameter = The Expensive Part**
- Increasing diameter improves:
  - **Collecting area** (∝ D²)
  - **Resolution** (∝ 1/D)
This is why large mirrors are **the costliest part** of a telescope.
**Typical sizes:**
- Largest optical telescopes in operation: **≈10 m diameter**
- Next generation:
  - **Thirty Meter Telescope (TMT)** → D = 30 m  
    - Collecting area increases by **factor of 9** compared to a 10 m mirror.
    - Canada (and Waterloo) are involved in its development.
---
**Magnification ≠ Telescope Quality**
- Magnification is determined by **secondary lenses/eyepieces**, not the primary mirror.
- It’s *not* a fundamental or expensive property.
- A small cheap telescope can have high magnification, but still perform poorly if it lacks:
  - collecting area  
  - resolution  

---
**Summary Table**

| Property | Determined By | What It Improves | Why It Matters |
|---------|---------------|------------------|----------------|
| **Light-Collecting Area** | Mirror diameter (D²) | Ability to see faint objects | More photons → better signal |
| **Angular Resolution** | Diameter (1/D) | Ability to see fine details | Separates closely spaced features |

These two characteristics—**brightness sensitivity** and **fine detail**—are what make big telescopes powerful.

---

**What Is Angular Resolution?**
- **Angular separation** = angle between lines of sight to two objects.
- If objects are **far away**, their angular separation is **small** → they blur together.
- If objects are **close**, angular separation is **larger** → easier to distinguish.
- **Angular resolution** = the **minimum angular separation** a device (eye/telescope) can distinguish.

---
 **Why Angular Resolution Matters**
- Determines whether you see **two separate objects** or just **one blurred point**.
- Example: Car headlights look like one far away → resolve into two as the car approaches.

---
**Wave Nature of Light & Resolution Limit**
- Light behaves as a **wave**, so it creates **diffraction patterns**.
- Diffraction causes **blurring** → sets the **ultimate limit** to resolution.
- Even stars appear as tiny **disks** (diffraction patterns) in perfect telescopes.

---
**Diffraction Limit Formula**
$\theta \approx 1.22 \frac{\lambda}{D}$
Where:  
- $\theta$ = minimum resolvable angle (in **radians**)  
- $\lambda$ = wavelength of light  
- $D$ = diameter of telescope mirror/lens  
 **Conversions**
- $2\pi$ radians = **360°**  
- 1° = 60 arcminutes = 3600 arcseconds  
---
**How to Improve Angular Resolution**
 **Increase $D$** — Bigger Telescope
- Larger apertures → **smaller** minimum angle → **better** resolution.
- Reason: Large telescopes sample **more wavelengths** → better direction detection.
**Decrease $\lambda$** — Use Shorter Wavelength Light
- Shorter wavelengths diffract less → better resolution.
---
 **Key Takeaways**
- **Resolution ∝ 1/D** → bigger telescope = better resolution.  
- **Resolution ∝ λ** → longer wavelengths = worse resolution.  
- Space telescopes like **Hubble** are **diffraction-limited** because they're above the atmosphere.
---
**Two Main Types of Telescopes**
- **Refracting telescopes (refractors)** → focus light using **lenses**  
- **Reflecting telescopes (reflectors)** → focus light using **mirrors**
---
**Refracting Telescopes (Refractors)**
**How They Work**
- Starlight enters → **objective lens** bends (refracts) light → forms image at **focal plane**
- **Eyepiece** magnifies this image for your eye  
  - Or you can replace eyepiece with a **CCD/photographic plate** to record images
- The image is **upside down**, but that doesn’t matter in astronomy
 **Pros**
- Simple design  
- Good for small, portable amateur scopes  
- Sealed tube → less dust, less maintenance
**Cons**
- **Very long tubes** needed for long focal lengths  
- Large lenses must be **thick and heavy**  
- Hard and expensive to build large high-quality lenses  
- Chromatic aberration (color fringing) unless corrected
 **Real Example**
- Largest operational refractor (in the UK)  
- Very large, very long tube
---
 **Reflecting Telescopes (Reflectors)**
 **How They Work**
- Starlight hits a **primary mirror** → reflects to a focus  
- Secondary mirror redirects light to a convenient viewing point  
- Mirrors provide most of the focusing power (lenses optional)
---
**Common Reflector Designs**
 **1. Newtonian Focus (Isaac Newton)**
- Primary mirror focuses light → small **secondary mirror** redirects it to the side  
- Eyepiece or CCD placed at the side of the telescope  
- Simple and widely used in amateur astronomy
 **2. Cassegrain Focus**
- Primary mirror → secondary mirror → sends light **back through a hole** in the primary  
- Eyepiece/CCD at the back of the telescope  
- Very common in professional telescopes
 **3. Nasmyth / Coude Focus**
- Light redirected sideways to a fixed location  
- Good for heavy instruments that can’t move with the telescope
---
 **Why Reflectors Dominate Modern Astronomy**
**Mirrors Are Easier to Build**
- Only the **surface** matters  
- Mirrors can be **thin and lightweight**  
- Lenses must be thick → heavy → expensive → impractical when large
 **Large Apertures Are Possible**
- Modern mirrors can be HUGE  
- Can use **segmented mirrors**:
  - Dozens of small hexagonal mirrors fit together (like a mosaic)
  - Much easier than building one giant curved mirror
**Better for Scientific Imaging**
- Astronomers rarely look through the telescope  
- CCDs placed at the focal point produce far better scientific measurements
---
**Examples of Modern Reflecting Telescopes**
- **Keck Telescopes** (Mauna Kea, Hawaii)  
  - 10-meter **segmented** primary mirrors  
  - Among the largest operational telescopes  
  - Hexagonal mirror segments combine to form one huge collecting surface

---
 **Imaging (Taking Pictures)**
**How Imaging Works**
- Similar to phone photography, but:
  - Astronomical objects are **extremely faint**
  - Observers use **long exposures** to collect more photons
  - More photons = **brighter**, **clearer**, **more detailed** images
**Color Imaging**
- Detectors record **one color (wavelength band)** at a time
- Use multiple filters:
  - Blue filter → take image  
  - Green filter → take image  
  - Red filter → take image  
- Combine images on a computer → **false-color or approximate true-color image**
- Filters have **wide wavelength ranges**, not single wavelengths
**Beyond Visible Light**
- Telescopes can image **X-rays, infrared, ultraviolet**, etc.
- Human eyes can’t see these wavelengths → colors are **assigned artificially**
  - Low-energy X-rays → colored red  
  - Medium → green  
  - High → blue  
- These are **false colors** used to represent data clearly
---
**Spectroscopy (Breaking Light Into Spectra)**
 **Purpose**
- Learn physical properties of objects from their **wavelength signatures**
- Determine:
  - Chemical composition  
  - Temperature  
  - Velocity (Doppler shift)  
  - Density  
  - Redshift  
 **How It Works**
- Add a **spectrograph** at the telescope’s focal plane
- Light hits a **diffraction grating**
  - Different wavelengths diffract at different angles
  - Produces a spread-out **spectrum** across the detector
- Similar to how a **prism** or **rainbow** separates colors
**Spectral Resolution**
- **Low resolution:**  
  - Features blur together  
  - Broad absorption seen, but details unclear  
- **High resolution:**  
  - Many narrow absorption lines visible  
  - Allows precise identification of atoms/molecules
---
**Time Monitoring (Tracking Brightness Over Time)**
 **What It Measures**
- How an object's brightness changes → forms a **light curve**
- Useful for:
  - Variable stars  
  - Supernovae  
  - Eclipsing binaries  
  - Planet transits  
  - Quasars  
  - Any object that changes over minutes → years
**Light Curves**
- A plot of **brightness vs. time**
- Different objects vary on different timescales (hours, days, years)
---
 **How the Atmosphere Messes With Astronomy**
Ground-based telescopes must look **through Earth’s atmosphere**, which causes major problems:
 **Atmospheric Effects**
- **Refraction**: Light bends unpredictably.
- **Turbulence**: Moving air causes image blur (the reason stars twinkle).
- **Weather**: Clouds, wind, humidity distort images.
- **Light pollution**: Scattered city lights overpower faint astronomical objects.
---
**Ideal Conditions for Ground-Based Observing**
 **Calm**
- Less wind → less turbulence → sharper images.
 **High Altitude**
- Less atmosphere to look through.
 **Dark**
- Far from cities to avoid light pollution.
 **Dry**
- Moisture refracts/scatters light and absorbs infrared.
**Best sites:** Remote mountaintops like **Mauna Kea, Hawaii**.
---
 **Adaptive Optics (AO)**
A powerful technique to correct atmospheric blur **in real time**.
 **How It Works**
- Telescope mirrors have **actuators** that constantly reshape the mirror surface.
- Adjustments cancel out atmospheric distortions.
**Effectiveness**
- Without AO → stars blur into a single blob  
- With AO → distinct sources resolved sharply  
- Atmosphere blurs images to ~**1 arcsecond**  
- AO can restore resolution to better than **0.1 arcseconds**
**Famous Use Case**
- Nobel Prize–winning work tracking stars orbiting the **black hole at our galaxy’s center** used infrared telescopes with adaptive optics.
---
**Atmospheric Transmission is Limited**
Only two wavelength bands reach the ground:
1. **Visible light**
2. **Radio waves**
Everything else is mostly **blocked**:
- Infrared → heavily absorbed by water vapor  
- Ultraviolet → blocked by ozone  
- X-rays & gamma rays → blocked by upper atmosphere  
Therefore, we need **space telescopes**.
---
 **Major Space Telescopes (by wavelength)**
 
| Telescope | Wavelengths | Notes |
|----------|-------------|-------|
| **Hubble Space Telescope (HST)** | Visible, near-UV, near-IR | Most famous optical space telescope |
| **JWST** | Infrared | Hubble’s successor, huge segmented mirror |
| **Planck** | Microwave | Cosmic background measurements |
| **Chandra** | X-ray | High-resolution X-ray imaging |
| **Swift** | UV & gamma ray | Gamma-ray bursts |
| **Fermi** | High-energy gamma ray | Particle astrophysics |

---
**Why JWST Looks Different**
- Uses **segmented hexagonal mirrors** (like large ground telescopes)
- Works mainly in **infrared**
- No need for a long “tube” in space → open design
- Huge sunshield + solar panels
---
 **Radio Telescopes**
 **Standard Satellite Dish**
- Works like a **small radio telescope**.
- Detects **radio waves**.
- Low resolution because radio wavelengths are **very long**.
 **Arecibo (Collapsed 2020)**
- Previously the largest **single-dish** radio telescope.
- Metal mesh is enough—radio waves are long, so the surface doesn’t need to be optically smooth.
- Fixed dish → can only observe near the **zenith**.
- Earth’s rotation helps sweep across the sky.
 **FAST (China)**
- Currently the **largest single-dish** radio telescope.
- Similar concept to Arecibo.

---
 **Infrared & Ultraviolet Observatories**
 **SOFIA (Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy)**
- **Infrared**, not UV.
- A telescope mounted inside a modified Boeing 747.
- Flies above most of the **water vapor**, which blocks infrared.
- Retired in 2022.
**Spitzer Space Telescope**
- **Far infrared**.
- Needed to be in **space** because far-IR is absorbed by Earth’s atmosphere.
 **Why IR & UV Need Altitude or Space**
- **UV is blocked** by ozone.
- **IR is blocked** by water vapor.

---
 **X-Ray Telescopes**
 **Chandra (NASA)**
**XMM-Newton (ESA)**
- X-rays cannot reflect off normal mirrors.
- Use **grazing-incidence mirrors** (Wolter optics).
  - X-rays skim the surface at shallow angles → focus to a point.
- Must be **above the atmosphere**, since X-rays don’t reach the ground.
---
**Gamma-Ray Telescopes**
 **Fermi Gamma-Ray Space Telescope**
- Gamma rays have **very high energy**.
- Not focused with mirrors.
- Instead they use:
  - **Detector crystals** that produce light when gamma rays interact.
  - Multi-layer particle detectors.
---
**Beyond Light: New Astronomy**
 **LIGO (Gravitational Waves)**
- Detects **ripples in spacetime**, not EM radiation.
- Opened a new field of astronomy ~2015.
**Other Non-Light Messengers**
- **Neutrinos** (IceCube)
- **Cosmic rays**
---
 **Interferometry (Radio & Beyond)**
- Combines signal from **multiple telescopes** → acts like one enormous telescope.
- Increases **angular resolution**, not light-collecting area.
- Works best with **radio** because:
  - Frequencies are lower.
  - Data rates are manageable.
 **Event Horizon Telescope (EHT)**
- Global network of radio telescopes.
- Produced the first **image of a black hole**.
- Uses **very long baseline interferometry (VLBI)**.
