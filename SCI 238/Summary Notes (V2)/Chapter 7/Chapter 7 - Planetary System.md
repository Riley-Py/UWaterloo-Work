 **How the Solar System Is Structured**
**Eight Major Planets**
- Today’s official list includes **8 planets** with **nearly circular orbits**:
  - Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
  - Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
- Most orbit in the **same direction** and within the **same orbital plane** (the ecliptic).
 **What Happened to Pluto?**
- When many of us were kids, Pluto was counted as the **9th planet**.
- Newer definitions (IAU, 2006) classify Pluto as a **dwarf planet**.
- **Definition of a dwarf planet:**
  1. Big enough for gravity to pull it into a **round shape**.
  2. **Orbits the Sun**.
  3. Has **not cleared its orbital neighborhood** (i.e., it doesn't gravitationally dominate its orbit).
 **Dwarf Planets**
- Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, Ceres, and a growing list of others.
- Often have **more elliptical** and/or **more inclined** orbits.

 **Why Compare Planets? (Comparative Planetology)**
 **Purpose**
- Understanding Earth in context by comparing it with **other planets and moons**.
- Helps identify **universal processes** instead of focusing only on Earth-specific facts.
 **What We Can Learn**
- **Climate evolution:** Venus shows runaway greenhouse effects; Mars shows atmospheric loss.
- **Geological activity:** Volcanoes on Mars, Io’s extreme volcanism, cryovolcanism on icy moons.
- **Habitability:** Conditions for water, atmosphere retention, magnetic fields.
- **Formation of planetary systems:** Comparing properties reveals how solar systems form and evolve.
**Why This Matters**
- Earth provides just **one snapshot in time**.
- Other planets act as:
  - **Past analogs** (what Earth may have once been)
  - **Future analogs** (what Earth might become)
  - **Extreme cases** that test our theories
 **Big Picture Benefit**
- These patterns reveal:
  - How the **solar system formed**
  - Why planets differ so much
  - How to interpret **exoplanets** in other star systems
  - What processes shape planetary surfaces, atmospheres, climates, and interiors

**The Sun**
- **Largest object** in the Solar System (99.9% of mass)
- Composition: ~75% Hydrogen, ~25% Helium
- Energy: Converts ~4 million tons of mass into energy per second (nuclear fusion)
- Features:
  - Sunspots (cooler, visible with filters or projection)
  - Solar activity visible in **UV** (chromosphere, hot gas)
  - Solar weather: flares, prominences, coronal mass ejections
---
 **Mercury**
- Closest planet to the Sun
- **Composition:** Rock & metal, large iron core → high density
- Surface: Desolate, cratered, steep cliffs
- Temperature:
  - Day: extremely hot
  - Night: extremely cold
- Atmosphere: very thin → cannot retain heat
- Rotation: long days & nights (~58 Earth days per day)
---
**Venus**
- Nearly **Earth-sized** (slightly smaller)
- Surface hidden by **thick clouds**
- Extreme **greenhouse effect** → hottest planet (~470°C)
  - Sunlight enters, infrared cannot escape → heats surface uniformly
- Atmosphere: very dense, mostly CO₂
- Recent news: NASA approved **two new Venus missions**
---
 **Earth**
- **Only known planet with life**
- Features:
  - Oceans, ice caps
  - Moderate atmosphere rich in oxygen
- Moon: **large relative to planet size**
  - Moon ~¼ diameter of Earth
  - Distance ~30× Earth’s diameter (~300,000 km)
- Atmosphere and water important for **heat retention** and habitability
---
**Mars**
- Similar to Earth in some ways, but:
  - Much thinner atmosphere → minimal greenhouse effect
  - Surface: giant volcanoes, huge canyons, polar ice caps
- Evidence of past water flow on surface
- Possibility of **subsurface microbial life**
- Atmosphere: mostly CO₂, very low oxygen
- Day length: similar to Earth (~24.6 hours)
---
**Key Comparative Notes**
- **Greenhouse effect:** Venus >> Earth > Mars (Mercury negligible)
- **Atmosphere:** controls temperature extremes and habitability
- **Surface activity:** varies with size, composition, and atmosphere
- **Moons:** Earth has unusually large moon relative to planet size
---
**Jupiter**
- **Type:** Gas giant (mostly hydrogen & helium)
- **Mass:** ~300× Earth → largest planet in Solar System
- **No solid surface**
- **Features:**
  - Rings: faint, recently discovered
  - Dozens of moons
- **Galilean Moons:**
  1. **Io:** Active volcanoes, tidally heated by Jupiter
  2. **Europa:** Possible subsurface ocean
  3. **Ganymede:** Largest moon in the Solar System
  4. **Callisto:** Large, cratered ice ball
- Weather: prominent **Great Red Spot** (giant storm)
---
 **Saturn**
- **Type:** Gas giant
- **Rings:** Most spectacular, visible even with binoculars  
  - Made of countless ice & rock chunks, not solid
- **Moons:** Many, largest is **Titan** (landed on by spacecraft)
- Weather: storms and turbulence, smaller than Jupiter’s red spot
---
 **Uranus**
- **Type:** Ice giant (H, He, water, ammonia, methane)
- **Unique Feature:** Extreme axis tilt → rotates on its side
- **Moons:** Many
- Rings: Present, like other gas giants
---
 **Neptune**
- **Type:** Ice giant
- **Similar to Uranus**, but normal axial tilt
- **Moons:** Many, largest is **Triton**
- Weather: visible patterns, strong winds, storms
---
 **Dwarf Planets**
- **Small, icy/comet-like composition**
- **Far from the Sun**, mostly in Kuiper Belt
- **Examples:**
  - **Pluto:** demoted from major planet, studied by **New Horizons**
    - Has surprising geology and patterns
  - **Charon:** Pluto’s moon, almost same size
  - **Eris:** another distant dwarf planet
- **Characteristics:** round shape, small mass, not gravitationally dominant
---
**Ordinary Motion of Large Bodies**
- All large bodies orbit the Sun in **same direction** (counterclockwise if north is up)  
- Orbits are **nearly circular** and lie in nearly the same plane  
- Most planets **rotate in the same direction** as their orbit  
- Exceptions: e.g., **Uranus** (tilted axis), **Venus** (retrograde rotation)

---
 **Two Major Planet Types**
**Terrestrial (rocky) planets**
- Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars  
- **Small size, dense**  
- Made of rock & metal  
- Few or no moons  
- No rings  
- Close to the Sun  
 **Jovian (gaseous) planets**
- Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune  
- **Large size, low density**  
- Mostly hydrogen, helium, and compounds (H₂O, CH₄, NH₃)  
- Many moons  
- Rings present  
- Far from the Sun  
---
 **Swarms of Smaller Bodies**
- **Asteroids:** mostly between Mars and Jupiter  
  - Can cross Earth's orbit → potential impact hazard  
- **Comets:** mostly in the Kuiper Belt (beyond Neptune) and Oort Cloud (very far, elongated orbits)  
- These small bodies preserve information about **early solar system conditions**  
---
 **Exceptions to Patterns**
- Some moons or planets deviate from general trends  
  - Example: **Earth’s large moon**  
  - Example: **Uranus’s tilted axis**  
- Indicates collisions, gravitational interactions, or other dynamic events in history  
---
 **Historical Measurement: Transit of Venus**
- Venus passes in front of the Sun → appears as a small spot  
- Observers at different locations see **different trajectories**  
- By measuring angular differences and knowing Earth’s size, we can calculate:  
  - Distance from Earth to Venus  
  - Astronomical Unit (AU) using Kepler’s laws  
- Last transit: **June 6, 2012**  
- Historical significance: first accurate measurement of **solar system scale**

**Observing vs. Sending Spacecraft**
- **Telescopes:** Observe planets from Earth or space
- **Spacecraft:** Provide close-up imaging, measurements, and sample return  
  - Advantage: direct study of surface, composition, atmosphere
  - Limitation: cost and complexity
---
 **Types of Robotic Missions**
 **Flyby**
- Spacecraft passes **once** by a planet
- Uses **gravity assist** to reach next target
- Example: **Voyager 2** (1977) – visited Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
- Advantages: low fuel cost, multiple targets
- Limitation: only brief observation
**Orbiter**
- Enters **orbit** around planet, moon, or asteroid
- Provides **detailed mapping and imaging** over extended time
- Example: **Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter**
- Limitation: cannot access surface directly
 **Lander / Probe**
- **Lands on surface** for close-up measurements
- Can analyze soil, rocks, atmosphere directly
- Examples:  
  - **Mars rovers** (Perseverance, Curiosity)  
  - **Venus landers** (short-lived due to extreme heat/pressure)  
  - **Apollo lunar landers**
- Uses **parachutes, rockets, and braking systems** to land safely
 **Sample Return Missions**
- Collects samples from surface and **returns to Earth**
- Requires **extra fuel and engineering** to launch back from planetary surface
- Examples:  
  - **Apollo missions** (Moon)  
  - **Hayabusa** (asteroid)  
---
**Notable Flyby: New Horizons**
- **Target:** Pluto (and Charon)  
- Launched when Pluto was a planet; reached Pluto as a **dwarf planet**
- Revealed **unexpected geological activity** on Pluto’s surface
- Example of **long-distance robotic exploration**
---
**Why Robotic Control is Necessary**
- **Speed-of-light delay** prevents real-time control
  - Mars: a few minutes one-way communication
  - Jupiter: ~1 hour one-way
- Spacecraft must **autonomously operate** using pre-programmed instructions
- Decisions made on-board for navigation, science operations, and safety
