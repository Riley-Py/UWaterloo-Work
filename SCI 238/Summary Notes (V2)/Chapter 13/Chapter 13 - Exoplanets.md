
 **Background**
- **Exoplanets** are planets outside our solar system.
- Field exploded ~25 years ago with the first confirmed discoveries.
- **Nobel Prize** awarded for the first discovery of exoplanets.
- Studying exoplanets helps us understand planetary systems beyond our own.
---
 **Challenges of Detecting Exoplanets**
- **Brightness contrast:**  
  - Sun-like stars are **~1 billion times brighter** than the reflected light from an Earth-like planet.
  - Only a tiny fraction of the starlight is reflected by the planet (depends on planet size, distance, and reflectivity).
- **Proximity to star:**  
  - Planets are very close to their stars compared to the distance from Earth.
- **Analogy:**  
  - Trying to see a pinhead next to a grapefruit a billion times brighter from hundreds of kilometers away.
---
 **Quantitative Example**
- Earth’s diameter ≈ 12,000 km, Sun-Earth distance ≈ 150 million km.
- Fraction of sunlight reflected toward Earth:  
  - Area ratio ≈ (12,000 / 150,000,000)² ≈ 10⁻⁸  
  - Taking imperfect reflection into account → roughly **1 part in a billion**.
- Makes **direct imaging extremely difficult** with current technology.
---
 **Historical Context**
- Early astronomers didn’t realize stars were like our Sun due to the huge brightness difference.
- Christian Huygens (1600s) estimated star sizes using drilled brass plates.
- **Parallax measurement** (1800s) was necessary to determine actual star distances.
- Angular resolution and telescope quality had to improve significantly to study stars as Sun-like objects.
---
 **Implications**
- Detecting exoplanets requires **indirect methods** (motion of star, brightness dips).
- Direct imaging is **very challenging** due to brightness contrast and small angular separation.
---
**Direct vs Indirect Detection**
- **Direct detection:** Imaging the planet itself or its spectrum.  
  - Extremely challenging for Earth-like planets: they are **~1 billion times fainter** than their stars.  
  - Possible in rare cases for very large or distant planets.  
- **Indirect detection:** Observing **effects of planets on their stars**.  
  - Much more feasible with current technology.  
  - Two main methods: **astrometry** and **Doppler (radial velocity)**.
---
**Astrometric Technique (Stellar Wobble)**
- Stars and planets orbit their **common center of mass**.
- Example: Sun & Jupiter:  
  - Sun wobbles slightly due to Jupiter’s gravity.
  - Center of mass is just outside Sun’s surface.
- **Observation:** Measure tiny oscillatory motion of the star in the sky.
  - Typical scale for a star 30 light-years away: ~0.5 milliarcseconds.
- **Tools:**  
  - ESA’s **Gaia spacecraft** has the precision to detect such small wobbles.
- **Limitation:** Requires **extremely precise angular measurements**.
---
 **Doppler (Radial Velocity) Technique**
- Measures **line-of-sight velocity** of a star using the **Doppler effect**:
  - Star moving **toward** us → blue-shifted light.
  - Star moving **away** from us → red-shifted light.
- **Physics:**  
  - Conservation of momentum: \( M_\text{planet} v_\text{planet} = M_\text{star} v_\text{star} \)
  - Star’s motion much slower than planet’s (e.g., Sun’s motion due to Jupiter ~1 m/s).
- **Observation:** High-precision spectroscopy detects shifts in spectral lines.
- **Information obtained:**
  - Period of orbit → orbital radius (via Kepler’s laws)
  - Amplitude of velocity → minimum mass of the planet
---
 **First Exoplanet Discovery (1995)**
- **51 Pegasi b**  
  - Detected via Doppler shifts.
  - Mass: ~Jupiter mass
  - Orbital period: 4 days
  - Orbit radius: 1/8 of Mercury’s orbital distance → “**hot Jupiter**”
- **Significance:**  
  - Challenged previous assumptions based on our solar system.
  - Revealed that planetary systems can be **very diverse**.
---
 **Summary of Stellar Motion Methods**
 
| Method       | Observes                  | Sensitivity / Tools             | Notes |
|--------------|---------------------------|--------------------------------|-------|
| Astrometry   | Wobble in star’s position | Milliarcsecond precision (Gaia)| Best for nearby stars |
| Doppler      | Radial velocity           | High-precision spectroscopy    | Most exoplanets discovered this way |

---
 **Transit Method**
- **Concept:** A planet passes in front of its star (as seen from Earth), causing a **temporary dip in brightness**.
- **Analogy:** Like the transit of Venus across the Sun.
- **Key points:**
  - Fractional drop in brightness ≈ **area of planet / area of star**.
  - Produces a **plateau-shaped dip** in light curve, unlike sinusoidal stellar variability.
  - Only works if the planet’s orbit is aligned with our line of sight.
- **Eclipse vs Transit:**
  - **Transit:** Planet blocks star light → noticeable dip.
  - **Eclipse:** Planet goes behind star → only reflected light is blocked → smaller effect.
- **Example:** NASA’s **Kepler mission** (2008–2018+):
  - Sensitivity: detects dips as small as 0.008% of star’s brightness.
  - Result: discovered ~4,500 exoplanets using this method.
---
 **Gravitational Microlensing**
- **Concept:** A massive object (star/planet) bends light of a background star, magnifying it.
- **Physics:** Prediction of Einstein’s **General Relativity**.
- **Effect of a planet:** Causes subtle changes in the magnification profile.
- **Use case:** Detects planets that are **far from their stars** or even free-floating planets.

---
 **Observing Protoplanetary Disks**
- **Concept:** Planets form in **dusty disks around young stars**.
- **Planet signature:** Newly formed planets can **clear gaps or create ripples** in the disk.
- **Observation:** Look for structures in **dust disks** to infer presence of planets.

---

 **Summary of Detection Methods**
 
| Method                  | How it works                                   | Advantages                     | Limitations                     |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Transit                 | Planet blocks star light                       | Can get planet radius & orbit | Requires alignment along line-of-sight |
| Doppler / Radial Velocity | Star’s spectral lines shift due to planet tug | Planet mass & orbit info       | Sensitive mostly to large/close planets |
| Astrometry              | Star wobbles around center of mass           | Detects distant planets       | Requires extremely precise measurements |
| Gravitational Microlensing | Planet alters magnification of background star | Detects planets far away       | Rare events, not repeatable     |
| Dust Disk Observations  | Look for gaps/ripples in protoplanetary disks | Infers planet formation        | Indirect; cannot measure mass directly |

 **What Properties Can We Measure?**
 **Orbital Properties:**
- **Orbital period (P)** – how long the planet takes to orbit the star.
- **Semi-major axis (a)** – average distance from the star.
- **Eccentricity (e)** – how elliptical the orbit is.
 **Intrinsic Properties:**
- **Mass (M)** – usually inferred via Doppler/radial velocity method.
- **Size (radius, R)** – inferred via transit depth.
- **Density (ρ)** – from mass and radius:  
	$\rho = \frac{M}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3}$
**Atmospheric Properties:**
- **Composition and features** – inferred from spectroscopy during transits.
- Example: a high density could hint at exotic composition, e.g., carbon-rich or diamond-like planets.
---
 **Doppler (Radial Velocity) Method**
- **Concept:** A planet tugs on its star → star “wobbles” → measured via **Doppler shift** in spectral lines.
- **Amplitude of velocity (K)**:
  - Proportional to **planet mass**: bigger planet → bigger wobble.
  - Depends on orbit shape: circular vs elliptical orbits.
- **Period of wobble** → gives **orbital period** and semi-major axis (via Kepler's laws).
**Circular vs Elliptical Orbits:**
- **Circular orbit:** Doppler curve is symmetric sinusoid.
- **Elliptical orbit:** Doppler curve is asymmetric:
  - Star moves slowly when planet is far.
  - Star moves quickly when planet is near (periastron).
 **Limitations:**
- **Mass–Inclination Degeneracy:**  
  - Measured velocity depends on **line-of-sight projection**:  
    $K \propto M_p \sin i$
  - Without knowing the orbital inclination \(i\), we only get a **lower limit** on planet mass.
- **Typical Doppler precision:** a few m/s for large planets, smaller for Earth-like planets.

---

**Summary of What Doppler Tells Us**

| Observable        | Information Gained                 |
|------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Velocity amplitude (K) | Planet mass × sin(inclination)  |
| Period of oscillation | Orbital period (P)               |
| Shape of curve    | Eccentricity of orbit (e)         |

---
 **TRAPPIST-1 System**
- **Seven known planets** detected by **Kepler** and follow-up observations.
- **Light curve analysis:**  
  - Each drop in brightness = transit of a planet in front of the star.
  - Multiple overlapping transits can be disentangled by computers to identify individual planets.
- **Planet sizes:**  
  - Range from 0.77 to 1.12 Earth radii.
- **Orbital periods:**  
  - Planets have varying periods (days) based on distance from the star.
---
 **Determining Density**
- **Mass:** from **Doppler (radial velocity) method** – measures star’s wobble due to gravitational tug of planet.
- **Radius:** from **transit method** – measures fractional dip in star’s brightness:  
  $\text{Dip fraction} = \frac{\text{Planet area}}{\text{Star area}} = \left(\frac{R_p}{R_\star}\right)^2$
- **Density:**  
 $\rho = \frac{M_p}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_p^3}$
- Example: TRAPPIST-1 planets have densities around **8.8–10 g/cm³**, suggesting rocky composition.

---
**Atmospheric Studies**
- **Transmission spectroscopy:**  
  - During transit, starlight passes through the planet’s atmosphere.  
  - Atmospheric molecules absorb specific wavelengths → absorption lines → composition.
- **Eclipse observations:**  
  - Planet passes **behind the star** → loss of emitted or reflected light.
  - Monitoring infrared light during eclipse can map **temperature variations** across the planet.
  - Example: TRAPPIST-1 exoplanets show temperature range of **~900–1200 K**.
---
 **Summary of Techniques**
 
| Technique                | What It Measures                            |
|---------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Transit depth             | Planet radius \(R_p\)                       |
| Doppler / radial velocity | Planet mass \(M_p\)                         |
| Mass + radius             | Density \(\rho\)                            |
| Transmission spectroscopy | Atmospheric composition                     |
| Eclipse / occultation     | Surface temperature maps, thermal emission  |

---
 **Orbital Properties**
- **Doppler-detected planets:**  
  - Tend to have **orbits smaller than Jupiter’s orbit** (closer to their stars).  
  - Detection is biased toward **massive planets**, because bigger planets tug their stars more strongly.
- **Kepler (transit) planets:**  
  - Can detect smaller, Earth-sized planets.
  - Orbits may vary; limited by mission duration (e.g., planets with very long periods may not have been observed fully).
---
 **Planet Mass and Size**
- **Doppler-detected planets:** mostly **Jupiter-mass or larger**.
- **Kepler planets:** mostly **smaller than Jupiter**, including **Earth-sized and super-Earths**.
- **Hot Jupiters:**  
  - Massive (similar or larger than Jupiter).  
  - Very close to their stars → **inflated radii** due to high temperatures.
- **Mini-Neptunes / Neptune-like:**  
  - Rich in hydrogen compounds.  
  - Less massive and smaller than Jovian giants.
- **Earth-like planets:**  
  - Comparable in size and mass to Earth.  
  - About **15–20% of stars** have at least one Earth or super-Earth.
---
 **Density**
- Exoplanet densities **vary widely**:
  - Some are denser than solar system planets (rocky planets).
  - Some less dense than water (puffed-up hot Jupiters).
- **Saturn example:** density < water → would float in a huge ocean.
- Diversity shows **mass and size are not tightly correlated**.
---
**Orbital Shapes**
- Many exoplanets have **highly elliptical orbits**.
- This contrasts with solar system planets, which are nearly circular.
- Indicates diverse formation histories and migration processes.
---
 **Key Surprises**
- Hot Jupiters: massive planets **very close to their stars** → formation and migration puzzle.
- Wide range of **sizes, densities, and compositions** → solar system is not necessarily typical.
- The universe shows **extreme diversity**, beyond solar system expectations.

**The Nebular Theory (Classical Solar System Formation)**
- **Basic idea:** 
  - Solar systems form from a rotating **cloud of gas and dust** (nebula). 
  - Dust grains collide and stick together → form planetesimals → form planets.
- **Predictions for our solar system:**
  - **Gaseous giants (Jupiter, Saturn)** form **outside the frost line**, where temperatures are low enough for volatile gases to condense.
  - **Rocky planets (Earth, Mars)** form **inside the frost line**, where only metals and silicates condense.
---
 **The Frost Line**
- **Definition:** Distance from the star where temperatures are low enough for water and gases to freeze.
- **Inside frost line:** Only rocky planets can form.  
- **Outside frost line:** Gas giants can form by accreting hydrogen/helium.
---
**Challenges from Exoplanets**
- Discovery of **hot Jupiters** (massive gas giants very close to their stars) contradicted the nebular theory’s expectations.
- Implication: gas giants can **migrate inward** after formation.
---
 **Mechanisms for Hot Jupiter Formation**
**Planetary Migration (Gradual)**
- Interaction with the **protoplanetary disk** (dust + gas):
  - Planet’s gravity creates **waves in the disk** → energy loss (gravitational “friction”).
  - Planet spirals inward gradually toward the star.
- Requires **massive enough disk** for friction to be effective.
**Gravitational Encounters (Violent)**
- Close interactions between massive planets or planetesimals:
  - **Scattering:** One planet is ejected, another moves inward.
  - **Orbital deflection:** Planets change semi-major axes abruptly.
- **Resonances** between planets can amplify these effects over time.
---
 **Observational Support**
- **Protoplanetary disks with gaps:**
  - Gaps observed in infrared images interpreted as planets clearing their orbits.
  - Motion of forming planets opens waves/gaps → supports disk-planet interaction theory.
---
 **Earth-Like Planets in the Habitable Zone**
- **Habitable zone:** Region around a star where **liquid water** could exist on a planet’s surface.
- For Sun-like stars:
  - Orbital period ≈ **hundreds of days**.
  - Planet size ≈ **Earth-size** (roughly 0.8–1.25 Earth radii for “Earth-like”).
- **Detection statistics:**
  - About **20% of Sun-like stars** may host an Earth-size planet in the habitable zone.
  - Caveat: surveys are incomplete, especially for **small, distant planets**.
---
 **Implications**
- Earth-like planets are **not extremely rare**, but not every star has one.
- Our solar system is **not necessarily unique**, but:
  - Hot Jupiters and other exotic exoplanets show **planetary systems can be very diverse**.
  - Systems exactly like ours (with small rocky planets inside, gas giants outside, and low-eccentricity orbits) might be **less common** than the typical exoplanet system
---
**Transit Surveys**
- **Goal:** Monitor hundreds of thousands of bright, nearby stars to detect **planetary transits** (dips in brightness).
- **Key Missions:**
  - **TESS (NASA):** All-sky survey for transiting planets around bright stars.
  - **CHEOPS (ESA):** Focused follow-up on known exoplanets to measure precise properties.
- **Future Focus:**
  - Detect **smaller planets** (including Earth-sized) around more stars.
  - Increase sensitivity to **fainter stars** and smaller transit signals.
---
 **Direct Imaging**
- **Challenge:** Planets are ~1 billion times fainter than their stars.
- **Techniques:**
  - **Infrared observations:** Planet emits more IR light, star emits less → better contrast.
  - **Adaptive optics / star blocking:** Special instruments block starlight to see nearby planets.
  - **Interferometry:** Combines signals from multiple telescopes to improve resolution.
- **Examples:**
  - Beta Pictoris system: several planets imaged after blocking starlight.
  - Systems with multiple planets detected in infrared.

---
**Astrometry**
- **Goal:** Detect planets by measuring the **tiny wobble** of stars caused by gravitational tugs.
- **Mission:** **Gaia spacecraft** is measuring stellar positions with unprecedented precision.
- **Future potential:** Direct detection of planets through stellar motion, complementing transit and Doppler methods.
---
 **Atmospheric Characterization**
- **During transits:** Light from the star passes through a planet’s atmosphere → absorption lines reveal atmospheric composition.
- **During eclipses:** Planet passes behind the star → changes in infrared light can map **temperature distributions** and thermal properties.
- **JWST (James Webb Space Telescope):** Will enhance the ability to study exoplanet atmospheres in detail.
