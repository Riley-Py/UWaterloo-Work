### Answer to Q1

Technical leaders are effective as not being the most knowledgeable people within the organization, as they aid in creating a culture within the organization that is iterative and is subject to feedback.  This allows individuals on the team to buy into the mission that the leader is trying to instil upon them.  An example of this would be within a software engineering team, where technical leaders aren't involved in C-Suite decisions, but are instead on the front lines, encouraging feedback about programming quality and taking that feedback to create better solutions.  As a result, individuals on the team are more likely to look up to the leader and respect them.  Thus, the claim that the most technical leaders are not necessarily the most knowledgeable people in the organization may be true, but it's severely lacking in regards to decision authority.  Technical leaders may not have control over what happens to their team, as they don't have the authority that the knowledgeable people within the organization - the C-Suite executives - have.  This can explain why some organizations are more innovative than others; some of them are agile and lean, while others rely on a traditional hierarchy structure.  As well, the way that technical leaders lead their teams can speak volumes as to the overall performance of the organization.

### Answer to Q2 

The full chain, from the system conditions, to the outcome of performance issues, starts with the work condition that the organization is in.  They are working in a highly specialized environment, which requires experts within that environment to be working in.  Thus, experts within the subfields of that environment are distributed about the organization in the goals of achieving productivity within the work environment.  The organization interprets this as a way of making sure that everyone is working to their level without any resources provided for communication for these subgroups, and puts in managers that are not technically skilled to manage these subgroups of experts.  This leads to fragmentation of expertise, where experts within their subfield can't cross-communicate with one another, as the managers are encouraging reporting to them, thus making communication harder.  This constrains the flow of progress that is being made on the teams, and increases stress on management, as they aren't getting information quick enough.  As a result, they demand rapid information that shows that the organization is doing fine.  This hinders participation, as employees know that any concerns that they raise will be suppressed.  Without any concerns, evaluation also stagnates, as it is biased towards one direction: that the organization is doing fine.  This leads to a disordered decision-making process within the organization, where multiple groups have to talk with each other in order to make final calls on products and what information to pass upward.  However, due to filtering and fragmentation, the coordination between the teams isn't in sync with what it needs to be - some teams are deciding on some things, while others are deciding with other things.  This leads to less cooperation between teams, and performance problems arise.  Expertise alone didn't guarantee performance, as they were placed on teams with non-technical managers who didn't know how to effectively coordinate technical people.  Authority and expertise diverged, as the managers were the ones making the decisions, but they didn't know the context in which the decisions they were making were causing ripple effects, which experts would have caught if they were able to review the information.  Some concerns weren't acted upon due to the filtering of information, the coordination not being there between groups, and communication being disjointed between specialists/managers.  Access to information influenced organizational outcomes, as managers had access to what management want, while specialists were only able to go through the managers and not to management.  Knowledge work complicated measurement as there wasn't any discrete measurement as to how people were doing and no feedback took place.  Outcome was system-consistent, as management thought that by putting knowledgeable people into management roles, they would succeed.  However, they didn't have the expertise to manage specialists/experts, which created the chain.
