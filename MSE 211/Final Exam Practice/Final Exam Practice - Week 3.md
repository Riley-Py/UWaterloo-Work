### Answer to Q1

The way that people perceive a decision as good or bad is subjective.  An example of this would be making an investment on a product that the individual thought would make them money, but the end result was that the product was worthless, and they weren't able to recoup the costs.  From an outsider point of view, they would attribute the outcome to the individual, as they were the ones making the decision.  However, this fails to consider the environment that the individual was in, which is supported by the readings through the attribution error, where people place more emphasis on the person's traits rather than the environment in which the person acted in.  To the individual who made the investment, at the time they thought it was reasonable as they were promised a great ROI on the product in the future without knowing about said future on the product; they made the decision with the knowledge that they had at the time.  Thus, the claim that most bad decisions are actually the result of earlier failures in perception and attribution may be true, but is limited by the fact that bad decisions can be the result of the individual's traits.  Going back to the example, say the person was going to invest in the product, but then someone from the future told them not to invest as they would lose money.  This person, being stubborn, thinks that they are wrong, and still makes the decision to invest in the product.  They were given all of the information available to them, but due to their trait of being stubborn, they made the investment knowing that they are losing money.

### Answer to Q2
The way the full mechanism can be explained to see the resulting outcome, from system conditions all the way through to behaviour, starts with working conditions.  There was a lack of safety built into the way people were working on the project, as management kept on demanding more and more features.  Additionally, management demanded to keep the project economically viable - meaning to make the project as cheap as possible - in order to receive a bonus.  Members on the team thus wanted to be more efficient when it came to the addition of the features, and to implement them in a cheap fashion.  The demands by management thus influenced members to perceive the project as a means to an end; if they were able to implement all the features and do it in the least costly way possible, then they would be awarded.  To an outsider, this perception, without knowing about the system conditions, would lead to a false attribution to the individuals and not of the environment, a hallmark of the attribution error theory.  The outsider, committing this error, then would judge the group project's failure as an individual failure based on the team members, and being tunnel-visioned on the traits of the individual.  The outsider, with this judgement in mind, would lambast the individuals on the poor quality of the project, the way the features were implemented, and the amount of errors in the final submission.    The behaviour appeared reasonable at the time as group members were under time constraints and were incentivized by management to get features done as quick as possible to make money.  This led to them skipping review steps that would have made the product better.   The failure of the project would be falsely attributed to individuals as the outsider would have only looked at the individuals without taking the time to go one level above them to the system conditions, like economic incentive or lack of safety in the system, that would have led the members to skip steps and thus increasing errors, resulting in a poor end product.
