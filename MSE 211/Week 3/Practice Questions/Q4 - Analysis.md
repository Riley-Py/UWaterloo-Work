Ross and Simon both describe limitations in how people interpret and act, but at different stages.

Identify:
- One specific way attribution (Ross) and decision-making (Simon) reinforce each other
- One specific way they are incomplete or insufficient if used alone

## Response
One way attribution and decision-making reinforce each other are the way constraints are formed.  After the perception of a situation, then in Ross' theory, the observer is tasked at attributing, and eventually judging, the way the actor behaved in said situation.  In the attribution theory, it describes how one can predict an actor's actions through their past behaviours, which can give insight into their judgement.  Similarly,  in decision-making, after seeing the situation and attributing further action to prediction, according to Simon, this prediction can be made by seeing the constraints that one has in the situation, which are based on what an individual can control and what has to remain constant.  An example of this in the real world would be a student choosing courses for the next term.  After seeing the situation of choosing courses, an outside observer may be able to attribute the reason why they chose specific courses due to predicting it in advance due to their past behaviour.  This prediction can be made due to how the student is limited in the courses that they can take due to the program they are in, and in the decision-making process, all alternative courses are evaluated before eventually making the choice of courses.  Again, this can go back to attribution, where perhaps we know the reason why a student eventually landed up choosing the courses they did - out of personal interest, the "lesser of the evil", etc.  However, if taken separately, then they are incomplete theories.  Without attribution, then there is no clear reason why a decison was made given constraints and after evaluating alternatives.  Without the decision-making process, then attribution simply can't happen, as you can't predict what hasn't happened yet.
